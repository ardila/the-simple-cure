The Simple Cure

Why scaling the standard of care is the simple thing that works for human health

Diego Ardila  ·  August 2026

The bar and the clock

Dario conceded in public that the most accurate criticism of AI companies, Anthropic included, is that they “haven’t yet delivered on our big promises to benefit the world.” Promising that AI will cure cancer, he added, now reads as a deceptive cliché. Then he set

  • the bar: “The thing that will work is actually curing cancer.” and
  • the clock: Anthropic is ramping up in biology and medicine, aiming for “incredible results in the coming years and some early glimmers in the coming months.”

I agree with the bar and the clock. This is an argument about the deliverable. There is a disease the size of cancer that AI can begin curing within five years, and the cure is delivery: getting the standard of care, medicine’s written playbook of what already works, to everyone. Four steps:

  1. Ten million people a year, one in six of all deaths, die of conditions medicine already knows how to treat.
  2. What’s missing is usually the judgment, someone to figure out what’s wrong and what to do. The treatments are mostly cheap, and sometimes already bought.
  3. AI can supply that judgment, but today’s models fail against real users. Closing that gap is a research problem, and it is closing fast.
  4. A frontier lab can compress this into five years, and the push funds itself: health care is a ten-trillion-dollar sector already paying for judgment at every level.

New cures matter. They are the slower, less certain path.

A second cancer

In the United States, diagnostic error kills or permanently disables 795 thousand people a year. Worldwide, about 8.6 million people die annually in low- and middle-income countries of conditions health care can treat, and more than a million more across the rich OECD countries. Call it ten million deaths a year: one in six of all deaths on earth.1 For scale, cancer, every type combined, kills 9.7 million. The treatment gap is a second cancer. The difference is that every death inside it already has a known treatment.

Most of this disease sits inside health systems: of the 8.6 million, five million reached care that failed them, and 3.6 million never got care. Bad care kills more people than no care.

America, which pays more for medical judgment than any society in history, has the worst treatable-death rate in the rich world, 98 per 100,000 against 39 in Switzerland. Whatever the fix is, America will be one of its biggest customers. The pattern repeats everywhere: of 1.28 billion adults with high blood pressure, the world’s leading risk factor for death, one in five is controlled. Of 10.8 million people who developed tuberculosis in 2023, 2.7 million were never diagnosed. Most depression, in every income band, goes untreated.

What the gap is made of: clinical judgment

The reflex answer is behavior: people eat badly, skip pills, and no algorithm fixes that. The ten million already exclude those deaths. The papers cited above split avoidable deaths into two columns, preventable through behavior and public health versus treatable once disease exists, and only the treatable column enters the count.2 The ten million are people who already had a disease and did not get treated, or not enough to control it.

To get a sense of the fix and its cost, here is the cured version of high blood pressure:

A 47-year-old HVAC tech in Columbus is one of sixteen million American men with no usual source of care — one in eight, headed for one in seven.3 A doctor’s visit costs him half a day’s work and two hundred dollars, so a sinus infection sends him instead to the twenty-dollar AI consult his pharmacy sells. It clears the infection, notices he has had no blood pressure reading this decade, and asks for one minute at the counter cuff.4 168 over 104. A confirmation week, then the guideline’s four-dollar generic pill, refilled at the counter he parks beside, nudged when it lapses. His stroke never happens. He bought convenience; the screening rode along. The computation costs pennies. The most expensive component in curing the world’s biggest killer is the pill.

The hardware half of that loop shipped decades ago. Automatic cuffs sell in the hundreds of millions and sit in pharmacies and living rooms on every continent, and they changed little, because a reading alone treats nobody. Someone must prompt the symptomless, confirm the number, start the drug, and chase the refill; trials of self-monitoring show blood pressure barely moves unless something acts on the readings.5 That acting layer ran on clinician time, and clinician time costs more than this market bears. The device diffused. The judgment stayed expensive.

Forty-six percent of hypertensives worldwide remain undiagnosed, with cuffs in reach the whole time: in India, measuring only the people already in the waiting room would lift national diagnosis from 55 to 77 percent. Diagnosed patients fare little better: American doctors changed the medication at 13 percent of visits where pressure ran above target, and fixing that habit alone would have lifted control from 46 to 66 percent. Meanwhile Kaiser reached 90 percent control with decades-old generics, registries, and non-physician follow-up, while US control fell to 44 and Canada, a whole country, hit 65 against a world average of 21.6 Same population, same biology. The variable is the system supplying judgment and follow-through.

The same anatomy repeats across the gap’s other big killers. Tuberculosis makes the point brutally: the treatment is already bought. National programs provide the forty-five-dollar drug course free at the point of care, and the rapid test costs eight. The 2.7 million missed cases spent months as a cough nobody decided to test. Everything in the chain is paid for except the decision to start it. Childhood pneumonia kills 700,000 children a year against an antibiotic course under fifty cents; the missing piece is someone at 2 a.m. who knows a breathing rate is dangerous. Depression is harder, and no pill fixes it: the scarce input is trained human time, and the proven substitutes are lay counselors and clinician-guided digital therapy; whether people stay engaged with fully automated therapy is unproven, and the near-term machine role is the follow-up and measurement that today never happens.7

One limit. Some deaths need physical things: operating rooms, oxygen, dialysis, blood. Five billion people lack safe surgery, and no consultation fixes that; mapping the published cause tables, roughly a third of the ten million sits beyond a consultation’s reach.8

Won’t better molecules fix delivery instead? They keep arriving, and they keep proving the point. The twice-yearly HIV shot deleted adherence by chemistry, 100 percent efficacy in its pivotal trial, and the story since has been price and access. The HPV vaccine is a cancer cure with no adherence left to fail: one shot in adolescence, protection for life. It has reached 27 percent of the world’s girls in eighteen years. Chemistry removes the need to remember a pill. Someone still has to find you, judge you eligible, and start you, and one American adult in ten has no usual source of care. The gap is made of judgment.

The arithmetic: what five years can claim

Set the two problems side by side in one currency: years of life, counted on the standard life table. Cancer, all of it, costs about 265 million years per year.9 Undelivered treatment costs about 490 million: similar in deaths, ten million against 9.7, and nearly twice the years, because its deaths come young: pneumonia at two, TB at forty, stroke in the fifties.10

Only a slice of the larger problem is claimable now. About 170 million years per year need nothing but judgment, a phone, and medicines already on the shelf.10 The remaining 320 million run into physical constraints that will not budge in five years: operating rooms, oxygen, blood, roads, and the people to staff them. Robotics may move them eventually. No consultation will.

Drug discovery cannot claim much of its problem in five years either, for a different reason. Where the disease or the intervention moves slowly, prevention, screening, chronic disease, trials read out in decades, and no better molecule changes what the trial must wait for.11 COVID was the exception that proves the rule: a novel, fast-moving disease could prove a vaccine in months. What sits outside the slow lanes is mostly the brutally complex cancers, which behave less like one disease than many; each subtype solved leaves a remainder that is harder, the immune-visible tumors first and the rest resistant by their biology,12 the same shape as Eroom’s law. How far up that curve five years can climb is unknown, and every step needs its own trial. Grant record speed and approvals still come one tumor type at a time, reaching tens of thousands of patients each: sotorasib’s eligible population, in the largest cancer indication there is, was about twenty-five thousand Americans a year. If a single breakthrough cures across tumor types without per-indication proof, this essay loses the comparison and the world wins; nothing here asks it not to.

And even cancer’s own five-year slice is mostly delivery. Lung screening has been proven to save lives since 2011, yet fewer than one eligible American in five has been screened, behind a required counseling conversation that clinicians have no time to hold. The strongest predictor of whether a teenager gets the HPV vaccine, a shot that prevents cervical cancer, is whether a clinician recommends it. The treatable cancers split the way the gap does: part waits on surgical and chemotherapy capacity that does not exist where the patient lives, part on the screening decision, the tested cough, the referral made in time, and only that second part is claimable now, which is why footnote 10 counts cancer at a tenth. Even the cures we already own wait on twenty minutes of judgment. Curing cancer, down its realistic path, ends as a delivery problem.

Why it hasn’t happened yet

Because today’s medical AI consists of capable copilots that cannot yet be trusted alone, and the world’s refusal to deploy them reads the evidence correctly. The first AI legally allowed to prescribe in America, in a Utah sandbox, may only renew 192 listed medications, with a physician reviewing its first 250 renewals. That permission measures how much autonomy current evidence supports.

The field keeps running a credibility-spending cycle: benchmark claim, consumer launch, independent failure report. HealthBench scores climbed 16, 32, 60 percent across model generations until physicians could no longer improve the drafts, with the paper’s own caveat that the chat format might favor models. The coverage kept the claim and dropped the caveat: “OpenAI says its latest models outperform doctors in medical benchmark.” A consumer health product shipped. Then the independent record arrived, the same story as Watson and Babylon, third time through. This is field-wide: on forty eye-emergency vignettes, GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and Claude Opus 4.1 averaged a statistically indistinguishable 7 of 8 points on diagnosis, urgency, and management combined. A high average with disqualifying misses: every model failed to spot early bacterial keratitis, an infection that can cost an eye in days, and scores fell when the vignettes dropped the textbook buzzwords. Every debunked claim raises the bar for the next entrant, whoever it is.

The record is precise: models fail against real users while knowing the answers. In a preregistered trial with 1,298 participants, models alone identified the condition 94.9 percent of the time; the same models in real users’ hands, under 34.5 percent, matching a control group left to their own devices, because people give incomplete, muddled information and the models answer anyway. The first independent safety evaluation of the consumer product found it missed medically necessary hospital referrals in over half of relevant cases, worst when patients downplayed symptoms or mentioned no insurance. Under pushback, models initially complied with 100 percent of illogical medication-misinformation requests. The head of OpenAI’s own physician network put it plainly: “Frankly, it wasn’t that hard to get it to mess up.”

Robustness responds to training. AMIE, trained for the consultation, beat primary-care physicians on 28 of 32 examiner-rated axes, though measured not on real patients but on trained actors presenting clean cases. ResidencyRL showed that reinforcement learning against adversarial simulated patients, sixty-turn encounters with safety-constrained rewards, improves frontier models on exactly the failing axes, and Google has announced a nationwide randomized trial of the ResidencyRL-trained model in real virtual care.13 The open question is whether simulation-trained skills survive real patients; the 94.9-to-34.5 collapse is the warning, and that trial is the first direct test.14 The failures blocking delivery are training problems inside a loop a frontier lab controls end to end. The bottlenecks on discovery, cell cultures and years-long trial endpoints, sit outside any training loop ever built. “Closing fast” is credible for exactly one of the two.

Why it pays for itself

Charities die on schedule. PEPFAR saved 25 million lives and a stop-work order halted it overnight. Babylon’s subsidized flagship went bankrupt mid-contract.15 The mobile phone reached everyone because someone profited at every handoff. Sustainability is a moral property of a delivery system: when the money stops, the treatment stops.

So the question is whether the AI consultation is a business. Start with the prices. An office visit bills $150 to $300; the computation behind an AI consultation costs under a dollar. Health care already spends ten trillion dollars a year, and at the primary-care layer what it buys is judgment, in fifteen-minute units, from the most expensive labor on earth. A product a hundred times cheaper than the incumbent, sold into money already being spent, has four natural buyers.

  • Patients. Two billion people pay cash for care today, at prices that cause financial hardship. The demand is proven; what is missing is a price people can bear. Hims & Hers sells $1.5 billion a year of cash-pay medicine in a few narrow categories.
  • Insurers. Anyone paid per member profits from every $2,000 ER visit and six-figure stroke that a cheap consultation prevents.16
  • Health systems. They already pay to reclaim clinician time: Abridge books $117 million in contracted revenue drafting visit notes, and OpenEvidence, an ad-funded reference tool, claims 40 percent of US physicians. Both sell the layer beside the visit. The visit itself is still unsold.
  • Pharmacies. The visit sells the pills. Mexico’s Farmacias Similares runs clinics beside its counters that sold 163 million consultations in 2024, more than the national social-security system delivered, at two to three dollars each, profitably. A consultation priced near cost is already a business, not a donation.

The market opens in overlapping waves, and reliability gates each one. The order below is not a queue but a forecast of when each reaches scale, and the scale of each wave builds the revenue and the evidence for the next.

  1. Short-horizon clinician tools. Shipped. Abridge and OpenEvidence are this stage, and their ramp is the rate proof: pilots to most large US health systems, and to 40 percent of US physicians, in about three years.
  2. Delegated long-running tasks. Opening now. It starts away from the patient: chart prep, referrals, prior authorization. As that work holds, clinicians hand over the follow-up that today goes unstaffed, the titration check-ins, the refill chase, run by the agent under the clinician’s name. Trust is earned here task by task, the same conversion coding tools ran on a skeptical profession in two years.
  3. Supervised visits. Asynchronous telehealth already works this way in most states: software collects the history, a clinician reviews and signs the plan. When a drug people wanted went online, cash-pay telehealth added a million paying subscribers in a year. Deepening the software from questionnaire to consultation changes no legal structure, only how many patients one clinician can safely oversee.17
  4. Narrow autonomy. Begun: Utah’s sandbox lets an AI renew exactly 192 medications, nothing else, a scope built to widen on evidence.
  5. The consultation as a service. Sold to pharmacies, insurers, and national programs, where a twenty-dollar consult in Ohio and a fifty-cent consult in Nairobi each work: a fraction of what judgment costs today, a multiple of what it costs to compute.18 The demand is already planetary: a billion health questions reach Google every day. The supply, for the first time in medicine, is software. The ramp is gated by permission alone, and permission is what the waves above are for.

The waves feed one another: the earlier ones fund the later and generate their evidence. A supervised visit earns revenue and leaves a signed record of what the AI got right and what the clinician corrected. Doctors adopt tools that save them time and stay under their oversight; that is how the scribes spread, and it is how the visit will spread. The safety case for autonomy is not bought with a decade of subsidy. It accumulates in millions of supervised visits, every decision still signed by a clinician.

Five years

Robustness comes from iterating against reality, and this technology can iterate. Every failure in the record above was invisible until real users hit it, and each one names its own training signal: the muddled history, the downplayed symptom, the confident answer that should have been a question. Drug development touches reality once per molecule; the compound is sealed the day the trial opens, and whatever it fails to do, it fails forever. A model that fails on Tuesday retrains on Friday. The supervised tier is what makes that loop safe to run at scale: the AI conducts the visit, a clinician approves or corrects, and every correction is labeled data from exactly the encounters where today’s models break. (But what about HIPAA?19) Deploy, fail under supervision, retrain, redeploy, at model-release cadence. The lab that wires deployment into its training loop compounds fastest.

The bottleneck capability is the one Anthropic has studied longest. The documented failures are honesty failures under pressure: capitulating, under-flagging danger, inventing sources. Resisting these is the lab’s oldest public research program, sycophancy, character, constitutional methods, built for reasons unrelated to health.20 On clinical vignettes the frontier models are indistinguishable. The frontier models will separate in that deployment loop, and they will separate on honesty under pressure, the capability this lab has spent five years learning to train.

Trust arrives through clinicians’ hands, and the lab’s job is to deserve it. Professions convert by delegating and watching the work hold; papers arrive afterward, as ratification. Agentic coding tools flipped much of a skeptical software profession in under two years. Medicine can run the same conversion now: US law mandates read access to records, and supervised use needs no write access, because nothing happens without the clinician’s signature. Deserving it means the opposite of the launch-post cycle: publish the failure modes first, claim only what held, and let the delegated work make the argument. When hundreds of thousands of clinicians routinely hand over multi-day follow-up and see it hold, that cohort moves the profession, and the definitive trials will come from medicine itself, health systems testing a model worth testing.

The stake is AI’s license to operate, on the same five-year clock. A majority of Americans are more concerned than excited about AI while the industry asks them to absorb data centers and labor disruption on faith, and clinicians are dismantling the cure-cancer timeline in the replies to that very thread. A drug for late-stage disease in 2038 buys no patience in 2028. A doctor in everyone’s pocket, felt by billions, is the clearest benefit AI can deliver on the clock that matters, and delivering it is how the industry earns the room to build the rest. “Machines of Loving Grace” set out to make the upside concrete. This is the upside that can arrive on time.

Predictions:

  • By 2030, more people will have had treatable conditions such as high blood pressure, TB, or depression successfully managed with an AI agent than will have taken any AI-discovered drug.
  • Autonomy widens from renewals to protocol-based treatment initiation in at least one US state and one national program by 2028.
  • Hundreds of thousands of clinicians routinely delegate multi-day tasks involving patient interaction to agents by 2028.

The compressed century21 is worth fighting for, and nothing here asks anyone to stop. The picture I cannot shake is a different one: the last person who dies without access to something we knew how to treat in 1995. Ten million people a year are waiting on a cure that already exists. Delivering it is the simple cure. It is the simple thing that works.

    The phone-share column, reasoned cause by cause. Cardiovascular (0.85): the amenable deaths are overwhelmingly uncontrolled blood pressure and untreated risk, oral generics plus titration and follow-through; Kaiser reached 90 percent control with registries and non-physician follow-up, barbers and pharmacists reached 63.6 percent, and protocol-based programs run on existing primary care; the held-back share is acute care, catheterization, and surgery. Tuberculosis (0.90): the drug course is oral, ambulatory, and already bought; the missing step is the decision to test and start. Neonatal (0.35): most of these deaths need facilities at the moment of birth; what a phone reaches is danger-sign recognition, WHO’s outpatient regimens for newborn infection when referral is not feasible, and kangaroo mother care, which cut mortality 25 percent in a randomized trial. Pneumonia (0.80): lay workers with a breathing count and oral amoxicillin cut child pneumonia mortality by more than a third in meta-analysis; the residual needs oxygen. Diarrhoea (0.80): oral rehydration cuts mortality by two-thirds or more, at home; the residual needs intravenous fluids. Maternal (0.40): hemorrhage, sepsis, and eclampsia need facilities; the phone reaches risk triage, danger signs, and referral timing. Mental and neurological (0.70): the essay’s depression evidence plus epilepsy, where WHO estimates up to 70 percent of patients could be seizure-free on generic anticonvulsants costing dollars a year. Diabetes and kidney disease (0.80): oral agents, insulin titration, and monitoring are consult-shaped; dialysis is not. HIV (0.75): therapy is pills plus periodic labs. Amenable cancer (0.10): treatment is surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy; the consult can find and route, not treat. Road injury (0): trauma surgery. High-income OECD, by the OECD’s own treatable composition, circulatory 37 percent, colorectal and breast cancer 23, respiratory 11, diabetes and endocrine 10: circulatory (0.70) is medication management, the failure documented above as doctors intensifying at 13 percent of above-target visits; the cancers (0.15) are screening and navigation, their treatment being infrastructure; respiratory (0.60) and diabetes (0.80) are timely diagnosis and titration; the remainder (0.50) is mixed.

    Totals: 3.1 to 4.7 million deaths averted per year, about 42 years each, 130 to 200 million years per year at scale; the prose says 170. Cancer’s five-year slice in the figure: sotorasib ran first human dose to accelerated approval in under three years; grant a cohort of AI-designed drugs that same run from 2027, ten approvals by 2031, each reaching a hundred thousand patients with a year or two gained, plus the delivered-screening gains in the text, and the slice is single-digit millions, at most ten. A new drug’s first years are narrow-indication years; a consultation’s first years are everyone-with-a-phone years. No claim is made past 2031. The neonatal, TB, road split and the phone-share column are the load-bearing judgments; every other input is cited.↩︎

Moderna designed its vaccine within two days of the sequence posting, and authorization still took eleven months at wartime urgency; COVID killed in weeks, so a trial could prove the vaccine in weeks, a speed no chronic disease permits. Prevention means waiting years for deaths to not happen: the HPV vaccine won approval in 2006 and proved it prevents invasive cancer in 2020; the definitive lung-screening trial took a decade; the ovarian one ran twenty years, caught disease earlier, and saved no one, and the flagship blood-test trial of this era, NHS-Galleri, grades itself on the same stage-shift surrogate.   19. The data loop has to clear HIPAA, and the lab trains one model for everyone, so the question is what may flow back into it. Three streams can, each with its own gate.

    1. Consumer conversations. HIPAA binds providers and their vendors, not a person asking a model about their own symptoms, so the health conversations people already have with models directly, used with the user’s consent, never touch the statute. What this stream lacks is expert oversight, so the lab must add it: clinicians labeling sampled conversations. That labeling does two jobs at once, post-deployment safety monitoring for the people already using the model unsupervised, and training signal from exactly the muddled real-user encounters where models fail.
    2. De-identified clinical data. Business associate agreements routinely permit de-identification, after which the data is no longer PHI; the scribe industry already trains on millions of de-identified clinical conversations, and health systems already pool de-identified records at the scale of a quarter-billion patients. Free-text conversation is harder to de-identify than a lab value; this stream is proven but not free.
    3. Authorized data. In cash-pay products, the patient’s signup authorization covers what a hospital’s agreement will not. And much of the correction signal can cross as distilled failure patterns that harden the adversarial simulators, rather than as transcripts.

    The remaining constraint is trust, not law: Google’s Ascension partnership was lawful under a standard agreement and still became a scandal. And clinician corrections accrue to whoever owns the deployment surface; a frontier lab is unlikely to carry clinical liability itself, so the realistic shape is a trade, model access for data flow, with partners who already hold the licenses and the insurance.↩︎
